# Market Analysis

## The window that opened in 2025

For nearly two decades, consumer installment loans in Brazil were a product for large companies. They existed, they worked, they had extremely low default rates, and yet they didn't reach those who needed them most. The reason was bureaucratic before it was economic: to offer salary deduction discounts, a company had to sign a formal agreement with a specific bank, a process that took 30 to 90 days, required a minimum volume of employees, and generated recurring administrative costs. In practice, this meant that a bakery with 22 employees would never have access to consumer installment loans, not because their employees didn't need them, but because no one was going to set up the agreement structure for a payroll that small. Less than 5% of SMEs with up to 100 employees operated with active consumer installment loans until 2024.

Provisional Measure 1.292, enacted in March 2025 and converted into Law 15.179 in May, knocked down that wall. The agreement was no longer necessary. Any CLT employee could now contract consumer installment loans through CTPS Digital, with the company merely processing the discount. The number that sizes the growth opportunity is eloquent: the balance of consumer installment loans was R$ 39.7 billion in March 2025, against R$ 322.6 billion in non-consignated personal credit [Genial Investimentos via InfoMoney, Mar/2025]. There is an entire market of people paying expensive credit interest because they never had access to cheap credit.

Regulation was a necessary condition, not a sufficient one. Two other things needed to mature at the same time. Pix reduced the cost of moving money: a transfer that cost close to R$ 5 per wire transfer now costs R$ 0.50 to R$ 1.50 [Paytime, Mar/2026]. In a R$ 200 advance, each real of unit cost equals 10% of revenue, so the difference between wire transfer and Pix is what separates a viable model from an unviable one. And the infrastructure of banking as a service matured to the point where a small fintech can obtain KYC, fraud prevention and FIDC management without needing a bank's balance sheet. The three things together reduced the minimum capital to originate microadvances from tens of millions to a few hundred thousand. Ágio would not be buildable in 2022. It is buildable now, and the window has less than two years.

The base on which all this operates continues to grow. In the fourth quarter of 2025, Brazil had 39.4 million workers with formal employment in the private sector, a record in the IBGE series, with growth of 3.7% in twelve months [PNAD Contínua, Dec/2025]. The mass of income in the period totaled R$ 363.7 billion, an expansion of 5.8%. More people formalized and earning more means more discountable salary, and discountable salary is the raw material of the product. The growth of the addressable market is nearly 9.5% per year without anyone changing behavior.

## The pain has two owners

The worker earning up to three minimum wages lives with a calendar mismatch. Bills are due spread throughout the month, salary arrives once, and when an unexpected expense appears (a medicine, a repair, a higher-than-expected bill) all options are bad. Credit card at 445% per year. Overdraft at 134%. Loan shark at 10% per month. Or ask the boss for an advance, which most SMEs refuse for fear it becomes a habit and creates labor complications. The pain is the lack of a bridge of a few days between the expense and payday, and the worker pays expensive rates precisely because they don't have access to cheap credit, not because they chose the expensive one.

What the Sprint 2 document left out was the second owner of this pain: the employer. The connection between employee financial stress and company loss is usually treated as something diffuse, almost rhetorical. It is not. Turnover in Brazil's operational base is high, and each termination has concrete costs that the SME owner pays without always realizing he's paying. Add the severance (notice, 40% FGTS fine, proportional benefits), recruiting the replacement, and the month or two of reduced productivity until the new employee learns the job. For a position at one to two minimum wages, the account easily reaches three or four salaries per termination. When part of turnover comes from employees who quit due to financial stress or who miss work because they're dealing with debt, the salary advance stops being a nice benefit and becomes a reduction in an expense the entrepreneur already has. This is the argument that opens the B2B side, and it's an estimate that still needs refinement with primary data from the accounting firms themselves, but the order of magnitude is enough to change the nature of the conversation with the SME.

## TAM: the theoretical ceiling

The total universe is obtained by exclusion. Start with 39.4 million CLT workers in the private sector and remove two groups that don't fit the product: those earning above three minimum wages, who have access to cheaper conventional credit, and informal workers, who have no discountable salary. Crossing IBGE data with IBPT income distribution, which shows 79% of employed earning up to three minimum wages, arrives at something between 31 and 32 million CLT workers within the target profile.

With a frequency of 1.5 advances per active user per month, a number that comes from Mêntore Bank's actual operations and was confirmed by Roberto Amaral, head of lending at QI Tech, in an interview to NeoFeed in February 2026, and average ticket of R$ 200, the TAM takes shape in three dimensions: approximately 567 million potential advances per year, R$ 113 billion in volume moved, and R$ 5.6 billion in total potential revenue if the R$ 9.90 fee applied to each one.

These numbers serve for one thing only: showing order of magnitude. No financial product converts the entire target population at the same frequency as a mature user, and treating TAM as a target would be dishonesty. What it answers is whether the market can support a relevant company, and the answer is that it can with plenty to spare. The interesting question begins at the next level.

## SAM: what the channel reaches

The SAM is where Ágio's strategic choice appears in the sizing. Three filters cut the TAM, each imposed by a model decision, not by accidental limitation.

The first is company size. Ágio targets SMEs with 10 to 100 employees, and this range is smaller than "small businesses" statistics suggest. Of 4.6 million small businesses opened in 2025, 77% are MEIs without employees and 19% are microenterprises with up to nine [SEBRAE, Dec/2025]. The target range, crossing RAIS, SEBRAE and the Central Registry of Companies, represents an active stock estimated between 500,000 and 700,000 companies, averaging 30 employees each. This puts the population at something between 15 and 21 million workers.

The second filter is income within those companies. SMEs this size concentrate basic operational functions (cashiers, attendants, auxiliaries, operators), which pulls the income distribution below the national average. Conservative estimates from RAIS suggest that 80% to 85% of these employees fit within the profile of up to three minimum wages, reducing the universe to 12 to 17 million.

The third is the channel. Most SMEs don't have an internal payroll department and outsource to an accounting firm. In companies of 10 to 100 employees, this percentage is close to 85%, excluding those that have already migrated to digital BPO solutions. The filter cuts another 15% and closes the SAM at 10 to 14 million workers reachable by Ágio via the accounting channel. Applying the same math as the TAM, this represents between R$ 1.8 and R$ 2.5 billion in potential revenue.

The SAM is an order of magnitude smaller than the TAM, and this is the direct consequence of choosing a channel instead of trying to reach the entire market. It remains large enough to sustain the investment, and it has the virtue of being a number that follows from explicit assumptions, not from optimism.

## SOM: what I actually capture

Here the document must abandon market logic and assume execution logic, because Ágio's SOM is not the piece of SAM that the product would theoretically conquer. It's what a solo founder, with R$ 103,000 in capital between personal contribution and angel investor, without a commercial team, can build by selling accounting firm by accounting firm. The SOM updated to my context is a SOM limited by resources, and this limitation is what makes the numbers honest instead of aspirational.

The acquisition curve in the financial model starts with nine accounting firms at the end of the first year, rises to twenty in the second and thirty-five in the third, with growth decelerating as the commercial effort of a lean operation reaches its limit. Each accounting firm, in full operation, adds an average of 1,500 CLT employees to the portfolio, and worker activation stabilizes at 30% after six months, a range consistent with the adoption that Gibb reports in mature operation [Exame, Feb/2025] and what Mêntore operates. The first year doesn't run at that full activation: it follows a curve that starts at 5% and grows month by month as accounting firms mature, which is the difference between projecting cash and dreaming about it.

The numbers that come from this are deliberately modest at the beginning:

Crossing the fifth-year SOM with the SAM, Ágio captures between 0.26% and 0.36% of the addressable market. It's a small slice in penetration and sufficient in absolute value to sustain the operation with positive margin. Comparison with adjacent fintechs confirms the pace is coherent: Mêntore Bank, with projected revenue in the billion-dollar range in 2025 and over five years of operation, serves companies with 500+ employees and grew through direct sales in a market three to four times larger than Ágio's. Gibb serves 100,000 employees with a target of 300,000 [Exame, Feb/2025], roughly ten times Ágio's base in similar timeframe, in digitalized medium-sized companies. Ágio is slower because the accounting channel has a longer acquisition cycle than direct sales, and the compensation for this slowness is higher retention and LTV per accounting firm. The slowness is a price paid in exchange for defensibility, not a defect of ambition.

## What the worker pays today

Ágio doesn't compete on price with large banks offering cheap credit to premium clients. The reference that matters is what the target worker spends today when they need R$ 200 before payday.

Ágio's implied annual rate needs a methodological caveat to avoid comparing different things: the R$ 9.90 is equivalent to approximately 60% annually if we assume a R$ 200 ticket and 30-day term, but that percentage drops to 24% on a R$ 500 ticket and rises to 79% on a R$ 100 ticket. What holds in any scenario is that, for the actual average ticket of the operation, the cost stays below all alternatives the base uses today due to lack of access to regulated consumer installment loans.

## Default is about the mechanism, not the product

Mêntore Bank reports 0.2% default rate throughout its entire operation. In a personal credit fintech without payroll deduction, the risk cost runs between 15% and 30% of revenue, and that's what forces these companies to charge high rates. At 0.2%, Ágio's effective risk cost is around 4% of revenue per transaction. The difference between 4% and 25% is exactly what defines the viable price range: without automatic deduction, Ágio would need to charge R$ 18 to R$ 25 per advance just to survive, which would throw it into the category of expensive products it proposes to replace. With the deduction, R$ 9.90 sustains the business.

It's worth being clear about what this implies, because it's a double-edged sword. Low default is a characteristic of payroll deduction, not Ágio. Any player operating with the same mechanism achieves similar results, which means the mechanism creates no competitive barrier on its own. Ágio's defensibility will have to come from the channel, and the operational flip side is that each accounting firm onboarding must guarantee the deduction works without friction, because any failure in the flow eats the R$ 2.77 margin per transaction quickly.

## What I still don't know

Three things in the sizing above are hypotheses, not facts, and pretending otherwise only delays the moment the problem appears.

The 30% activation rate comes from market references, Gibb and Mêntore, which operate in companies with quite different profiles from the traditional SMEs that Ágio targets. In smaller companies, where the worker's financial margin is tighter, the need for short-term credit tends to be higher, which can pull activation up. But it could also be lower if the digital trust barrier is higher than I estimate. There is no primary data with the target population, and Sprint 4 will generate the first, in conversations with workers from the personal network. Until then, 30% is the defensible floor, not a certainty.

The stock of SMEs with 10 to 100 employees is an estimate crossed from sources that report by income range or aggregate size, rarely by exact number of CLT employees. The range of 500 to 700 thousand companies has a margin of about 20% on each side, which doesn't change the SAM's order of magnitude or strategic conclusion, but needs to be stated.

And the competition from regulated consumer installment loans can accelerate. If large banks decide to enter small SMEs with automated agreements, part of Ágio's differential erodes. The risk is mitigated by these banks' historical inertia in the segment, which have high enough CAC to serve 30-employee companies with reasonable economies and sales structure optimized for larger customers. The protection is temporal and narrow, and it's part of why execution speed in the next eighteen months matters more than any other variable.

The market is large, growing at real macroeconomic rate, and has a regulatory window open for less than two years. The segment Ágio targets is underserved by capitalized players for a precise economic reason, not by accident: direct sales don't close the equation in small companies. The R$ 6.4 million SOM in five years represents less than 0.4% of the SAM, and all that slack says one thing only. What limits Ágio in coming years is not market size or direct competition. It's the speed with which a founder can transform accounting firms into a channel. Sprint 4 begins testing whether that transformation is possible outside the spreadsheet.

# Strategic Positioning

## Ágio is a distribution operation

The most precise way to describe Ágio is not as a salary advance fintech. It's as a distribution operation that carries a financial product inside. The distinction seems subtle and is decisive, because it organizes every choice that follows. The product, advance with payroll deduction, is validated by five years of Mêntore Bank's operation, is simple to build and is available to any fintech with minimum capital. What doesn't exist is a network of accounting firms distributing this product to SMEs of 10 to 100 employees. That network is the asset, and building it is the work. Everything Ágio does in the next five years derives from treating the problem as a channel problem, not a product problem.

This choice has three non-obvious consequences. The first is that capital goes to the channel, not to technology. The backend stays lean, Node with SQLite migrating to PostgreSQL when volume demands it, infrastructure via QI Tech, and most effort in the next twenty-four months goes to understanding, approaching, integrating and retaining accounting firms. The CAC per accounting firm and the payback of that acquisition, modeled in the financial spreadsheet, are the numbers that govern the operation, not code sophistication. The second consequence is that the moat isn't in the product, because any competitor replicates it in three months, but in a relationship network that can't be bought with funding. The third is uncomfortable and needs to be assumed: Ágio will grow slower than comparable fintechs selling direct. Gibb went from zero to 100,000 users in a few years through direct sales to digitalized mediums. Ágio depends on a slower channel where each accounting firm can take six to twelve months to mature. Slowness is the price of defensibility, and whoever won't accept the price shouldn't choose the channel.

## What Ágio refuses to be

Positioning defines itself as much by what you do as by what you refuse, and Ágio explicitly refuses five things.

It won't be a bank. Mêntore grew to the billion-dollar range by offering accounts, cards, working capital and advances as a package, taking over company payrolls. Ágio has no balance sheet ambition, doesn't issue cards, doesn't replace the worker's bank and doesn't ask the company to migrate their payroll. It enters the existing flow without demanding structural change, and this restraint is what makes adoption by the SME nearly painless.

It won't be a benefits platform. Caju, Flash and Biz build horizontal offerings, meal vouchers, transport vouchers, health plans, advances, sold as modules. Ágio does one thing, and adding products before consolidating the channel would dilute focus when focus is the only truly scarce resource.

It won't do direct sales to the company. This is the most important refusal, because it's where most fintechs in the segment went, and going there would weaken the proposition for the accounting firm, who needs to see Ágio as a partner not as someone who will bypass them and talk directly to the company. Sales are always through the accounting firm, and the company enters the conversation only to authorize the benefit.

It won't do mass marketing to the worker. Access is through a link with CPF, distributed by the company. There's no paid media, no direct user acquisition, and the cost of reaching the worker is absorbed by the accounting firm and company, who deliver the link. This is what keeps the worker CAC near zero.

It won't pursue unicorn traction in the first five years. The R$ 6.4 million gross revenue SOM in year five is small by growth venture standards, and this is a choice, not a limitation to hide. Ágio seeks sustainable operational break-even and compounded growth via channel, and this choice defines coherent capital profile: angel and seed, not a Series B forced in eighteen months to feed a curve the accounting channel won't support.

## The stakeholder who can say no

There's an actor in the model whose power Sprint 2 underestimated, and ignoring them would repeat an error that feedback already pointed out. The HR manager or SME owner has veto power, and it's crucial to distinguish carefully what that veto covers. They don't decide whether the worker will use the product, that's the worker's decision. They decide whether the product enters the company at all. If the owner thinks Ágio will create labor doubts, complaints at the counter or extra work for payroll, they block it, and because the entry door is the accounting firm serving that owner, their veto locks the channel for that entire company.

The practical consequence is that the HR or owner's influence is high at entry and low at traction. High at entry because without their yes there's no link distributed nor deduction processed. Low at traction because, once inside, the user who decides use is the worker, and the employer has no reason to interfere in a benefit that costs them nothing and reduces the turnover they pay for. Correct positioning treats the employer as gate guardian, not client, and the argument that opens that gate isn't the product's social impact, it's the hard number of termination cost that the advance helps reduce. Selling wellness to the SME owner is weak. Selling turnover reduction is what works.

## The three phases, anchored in what the spreadsheet says

Execution plans in three phases, and each must tell the same story as the financial spreadsheet, or positioning says one thing and the model says another.

The first phase, years one and two, proves the model works at small scale. The target isn't number of contracts signed, it's number of accounting firms in actual operation, with monthly cycle running and transactions flowing. The spreadsheet places operational break-even around ten accounting firms in full operation, equivalent to about 4,460 active users, the point where per-transaction margin covers expansion-phase fixed costs. The acquisition curve starts with nine accounting firms at year-end one, which puts break-even right at the turn into year two, consistent with the reading that year one burns cash and year two approaches equilibrium. The operational risk of this phase is confusing logo growth with operation growth, optimizing for partnership announcements and discovering months later that only a fraction of accounting firms generate volume. The honest metric is accounting firm with over a hundred transactions monthly, not accounting firm that signed a contract.

The second phase, years three and four, multiplies from twenty to something between forty and fifty-five accounting firms in full operation, in line with the spreadsheet's projection reaching thirty-five in year three. The challenge changes in nature. In phase one each accounting firm is integrated almost artisanally, with founder attention. In phase two, that must become a repeatable playbook executed by non-founder staff, demanding onboarding documentation, standardized materials for the accounting firm to use with clients, and quality metrics per implementation so scale gains don't become operational deterioration. The risk here is diversifying product before consolidating the channel, and the temptation to become a complete platform needs to be deferred to the next phase.

The third phase, from year five onward, is when portfolio expansion justifies itself, because the defensible asset is already built: network of active accounting firms, direct communication with activated workers, financial behavior data from the base, and recognized brand in the accounting market. On top of that asset we distribute consumer installment loans in partnership with a bank, simplified pensions, life insurance with low premium discounted in payroll, each one a separate decision made with real demand data. The economics of phase three is qualitatively different, because revenue stops being just transactions times R$ 9.90 and starts including cross-sell per activated worker, with LTV rising and marginal acquisition cost falling because the worker is already a client. The risk is starting too early and dispersing the team before the channel is deep.

## What builds defense over time

Ágio's defensibility is not a fact, it's an accumulation, and three mechanisms build it at different rates. The operational switching cost grows every month an accounting firm uses the product, because training the team, configuring the accounting software to receive the discount report and communicating to clients that the benefit exists takes four to eight weeks, and switching providers forces redoing everything. The incentive alignment through recurring commission keeps the accounting firm interested in operation success in a way single-payment models can't, because the firm's revenue is proportional to use, so they have reason to expand adoption instead of just implementing and forgetting. And accumulated sectoral knowledge, the difference in how Domínio, Totvs and Senior structure payroll data, the seasonality of monthly closing, the language of the owner and objections of the assistant running the upload, forms a learning curve that a new entrant traverses from zero. None of the three appears on a Series A deck, and that's exactly why they protect.

## The metrics that say if it's working

Five indicators reveal if the positioning holds, and all anchor in the spreadsheet. The onboarding time of an accounting firm, which can be four to eight weeks in phase one but needs to drop to two to four in phase two, or the playbook isn't repeatable. The activation rate per accounting firm, with 30% steady-state assumption, which if it stays consistently below 15% signals the product doesn't solve enough pain or worker communication fails. Accounting firm retention at twelve months, which needs to exceed 90% given high switching cost, and whose drop below 80% would signal something structurally wrong in operation or relationship. The LTV to CAC ratio per accounting firm, which the model needs to reach at least 5 to 1 within eighteen months to validate the thesis, and if it comes lower reveals overstated LTV or high CAC. And declining acquisition cost, because the first accounting firm costs much effort and the thirtieth should cost much less, and if that curve doesn't materialize the channel isn't becoming an asset, it's being worked case-by-case with no scale gains.

A sixth metric adds to these five, and it's not a business metric, it's a responsibility metric, and the careful investor will push for it: the income commitment index. If the worker advances 30% of salary every month, they're not using an emergency bridge, they're entering a debt spiral in disguise, and a business that thrives pushing the base into that spiral is unsustainable in image and regulatory risk before it's unsustainable in economics. Measuring how much each active user's income is committed to recurring advances is what separates a product solving a problem from one creating one, and Ágio needs to carry this metric from day one, even if it only gains statistical meaning after several cycles.

## What's still undecided

Four decisions depend on data only Sprints 4 and 5 will bring, and pretending they're already solved would be dishonest. Initial geography, whether to concentrate effort in São Paulo, which has the largest market and also 29,000 competing accounting firms, or start in a smaller regional market where density comes faster. Onboarding model, whether fully remote from the start, cheaper and more scalable, or in-person with the first accounting firms, more expensive but better retention and referral, with the likely choice being hybrid. Operation capital structure, between own capital plus angel in the first phase or FIDC from the start, knowing FIDC only pays above minimum circulation volume. And initial team composition, which the spreadsheet models as a developer and support person as first hires, but which the thesis of channel as primary asset suggests reordering, bringing a channel head earlier while the founder keeps coding for a few more months. The defensible sequence is developer first to free the founder from technical maintenance, channel head when there are at least three integrated accounting firms serving as reference for training, not hiring driven by who appears to be hired.

Ágio positions itself, in the end, as the first operation to treat the accounting firm as a distribution channel for financial products to small SMEs. The product is proven, the mechanism operates with low default, infrastructure is available. The variable that decides the outcome is how fast this channel transforms into an asset in the next eighteen months, before any capitalized player notices the window is open. Sprints 4 and 5 will confront this positioning with the reality of conversations with real accounting firms, and it's that reality, not the spreadsheet, that will calibrate decisions still in play.

# Financial Analysis

Note: The complete financial spreadsheet is available in the GitLab repository. It contains five tabs: Assumptions (input assumptions), Projection_12M (detailed monthly projection for year one), Projection_36M (annual projection for three years), Financial_Analysis (viability indicators), and Scenarios (sensitivity analysis with pessimistic, base and optimistic scenarios). This document explains the reasoning behind the numbers, the methodological choices and what each result means. To follow along, open the spreadsheet alongside this reading.

Every financial projection of a business that doesn't yet exist is a structure of chained hypotheses, and the honesty of a spreadsheet lies less in number precision than in clarity about where each comes from and what would happen if it were wrong. This analysis doesn't try to prove Ágio will succeed. It answers a more modest and more useful question: given an explicit set of assumptions, how long until the operation reaches equilibrium, how much capital it requires to get there, and what return it generates for whoever puts that capital in. The assumptions are debatable, and the spreadsheet was built so they can be debated, with each input variable isolated in a tab from which all others derive by formula. Change the usage frequency from 1.5 to 2.0 and the entire projection recalculates. This design is intentional, because a spreadsheet where results are manually entered instead of calculated is a spreadsheet that lies as soon as someone changes an assumption and forgets to update the rest.

The spreadsheet has five tabs, and they read in order. Assumptions feed the twelve-month projection, which extends into thirty-six, which in turn feeds the viability analysis, and the scenarios tab tests how much all this resists to more pessimistic and optimistic assumptions. What follows is an explanation of the reasoning, not a transcription of the formulas.

Before any projection, you need to understand the economics of a single advance, because it's that transaction repeated thousands of times that builds or destroys the business. The worker pays R$ 9.90 per advance, a fixed value regardless of amount. Of that total, 25% goes to the accounting firm as commission, R$ 2.48, leaving R$ 7.43 in net revenue for Ágio. Against that net revenue are direct costs: the FIDC capital cost financing the advance, estimated at R$ 2.46, plus Pix, infrastructure, KYC and allocated support, totaling R$ 4.66 in total direct cost. What's left is a gross margin of R$ 2.77 per transaction, equivalent to 37% of net revenue or 28% of gross revenue. The two readings describe the same number, and the distinction matters because gross margin without stating the base is an incomplete claim.

There's a known fragility in this calculation that needs stating: the R$ 2.46 capital cost is an estimate, because QI Tech hasn't yet confirmed actual infrastructure costs. If that number comes in above projection, the R$ 2.77 margin shrinks proportionally, and because the margin is what pays the operation's fixed cost, any error here propagates across the entire analysis. The spreadsheet treats this value as an input variable precisely so that when QI Tech responds, updating a single cell shows the impact on everything.

## The twelve-month projection and the ramp

The monthly projection tab is where year one gains texture, and it carries the single most important methodological decision in the spreadsheet. The temptation when projecting users is to multiply accounting firms by average portfolio size by activation rate and call it done. That grossly overestimates cash, because it assumes an accounting firm that closed in month twelve already operates with the same activation as one operating since month three. The spreadsheet models what actually happens: accounting firms enter over the year according to an acquisition curve starting at zero and reaching nine by month twelve, and worker activation within each accounting firm grows month by month, starting at 5% and rising until stabilizing at 30% after six months of operation. The result is that the average active users in year one is 1,638, not the 2,025 that the simple math produces. The difference between these two numbers is the difference between projecting revenue and dreaming about it.

With this actual ramp, year one generates R$ 291,812 in gross revenue and closes with negative EBITDA of R$ 58,599. Negative EBITDA in year one isn't a defect, it's the nature of any operation that must pay fixed costs before having the volume to cover them. The fixed cost, incidentally, also follows phases: R$ 200 monthly for the first three months, when the operation is just the founder with Claude Pro Max and hosting, rising to R$ 9,500 between months four and six with the first developer and support arrival, and to R$ 18,500 from month seven onward in the expansion phase. Cash doesn't go negative during the year because the initial contribution sustains it, and that's precisely what the contribution is for.

## Extension to thirty-six months

Year one is detailed month by month because it's the most uncertain and most under founder control. Years two and three are projected more aggregately, continuing the acquisition curve: twenty accounting firms in year two, thirty-five in year three. From year two onward the spreadsheet assumes 30% full activation, because by then accounting firms have matured, and calculates users based on the average of active accounting firms in the period. Year two generates R$ 1.162.755 in gross revenue with EBITDA still negative at R$ 35,251, and year three reaches R$ 2.205.225 in gross revenue with EBITDA finally positive at R$ 75.904. The trajectory is what matters to read here: EBITDA goes from minus R$ 58 thousand to minus R$ 35 thousand and crosses into plus R$ 75 thousand, a curve that tilts upward and accelerates, because fixed costs grow slowly while revenue grows with each new accounting firm.

## Break-even

Operational break-even is the point where accumulated gross margin covers monthly fixed costs, and the spreadsheet calculates it straightforwardly: the expansion-phase fixed cost, R$ 18,500 monthly, divided by the R$ 2.77 per-transaction margin, gives 6,691 transactions monthly. Dividing by the 1.5 advances per user frequency arrives at 4,461 active users. And dividing by the 450 users each accounting firm generates in full operation, break-even sits at 9.9 accounting firms, practically ten. Since the acquisition curve reaches nine accounting firms by year-end one, break-even falls right at the turn into year two, consistent with the reading that year one burns cash and year two approaches equilibrium.

It's worth noting this number corrects what previous documentation claimed. Old versions spoke of break-even in the range of 9,615 to 14,423 users, which never reconciled with the 1,500 employees-per-firm assumption, because it would require over twenty accounting firms to sustain. The correct number, derived from the spreadsheet, is roughly 4,460 users and ten accounting firms. When documentation and spreadsheet disagree, the spreadsheet is right, because it calculates.

## NPV and IRR

Here lies the part of analysis that most easily deceives the quick reader, and that's why it was built to be read slowly. Investment viability is measured by the net present value of the flows it generates, discounted at a rate reflecting the cost of capital opportunity, in this case 15% annually. Total investment is R$ 103,000, with R$ 3,000 from the founder and R$ 100,000 from angel investor. The question is which flows to discount, and the honest answer requires separating two questions usually mixed together.

The first question is whether the operation pays for itself in three years, considering only the EBITDA it generates in that period. The answer is no. The operational flows of the first three years sum to less than the initial investment, EBITDA is negative the first two years and barely positive the third, and the net present value considering only these flows is negative at R$ 130,702. The internal rate of return of these operational flows doesn't even exist as a positive number, because the investment doesn't recover in the horizon. The spreadsheet shows this explicitly, not hiding it, because hiding would be worse: any evaluator with finance training reconstructs this calculation in thirty seconds, and discovering it was omitted costs more credibility than admitting it upfront.

The second question is the one that truly matters: how much is the operation worth beyond the three-year horizon. And here's the point that changes everything. Three years is too short a horizon to judge a business that doesn't reach break-even until year-end one. Modeling only through year three is cutting the film the moment the operation just turned profitable, ignoring all the value it generates afterward, when positive and growing EBITDA repeats year after year on an already-paid fixed cost base. The terminal value captures exactly this: the present value of all future flows beyond year three. The spreadsheet calculates it deliberately conservatively, as five times year-three EBITDA, R$ 379,519, not through Gordon perpetuity, which at 5% growth and 15% discount would produce a 10.5x multiple and more than double the terminal value. The choice of the smaller multiple is a choice of prudence: I prefer a case that sustains on modest assumption to one that shines on aggressive assumption.

Including terminal value, NPV comes positive at R$ 118,838 and IRR at 41% annually, above the 15% cost of capital, making the investment attractive. But the correct reading of these two lines, operational and with terminal value, isn't choosing the convenient one. It's understanding what each says. The pure operation doesn't pay for itself in three years because three years is building time, not harvest time. The return comes from the post-break-even perpetuity, and it's only attractive if the assumption that the operation keeps generating growing EBITDA beyond year three holds. Showing both ends is what lets evaluators form their own judgment instead of swallowing a polished number.

There's also a third metric, return on investment, calculated as year-three EBITDA over total contribution, 74%. It was deliberately named return on investment, not ROE, because ROE in strict accounting sense is net profit over net equity, and EBITDA isn't net profit nor contribution is equity. Calling that ratio ROE would be imprecise, and nomenclature precision in entrepreneurship work is the kind of detail that separates someone who understands from someone repeating acronyms.

## Scenarios, and what they stress

The scenarios tab exists to answer the inevitable question: what if the assumptions are wrong. It varies five key variables between a pessimistic and optimistic case. In the pessimistic, five accounting firms by year-end one, 20% activation, frequency of one advance monthly, R$ 180 ticket. In the optimistic, fourteen accounting firms, 40% activation, frequency of two monthly, R$ 250 ticket. The base case sits in the middle, with nine accounting firms, 30% activation and 1.5 frequency, the number that comes from Mêntore Bank's actual operation confirmed by QI Tech.

Frequency deserves a note, because it's the highest-leverage variable and the most corrected. Earlier spreadsheet versions used inconsistent frequencies across scenarios, with the base case declaring 2.0 but calculating revenue at 1.5. The current version fixes 1.0 on pessimistic, 1.5 on base and 2.0 on optimistic, a coherent progression where adoption optimism also reflects in frequency, not just in accounting firm count. To ensure the scenarios tab talked to the main projection, user average calculations were aligned to the same ramp factor the twelve-month projection produces, so the scenarios base case generates exactly the same 1,638 users as the detailed projection. Before this correction, the two tabs disagreed on the base case, and two tabs disagreeing on the same thing is exactly the kind of inconsistency that undermines model confidence.

What the scenarios reveal is the business's sensitivity to execution. The distance between pessimistic and optimistic case is nearly fourteen times in revenue, meaning Ágio's outcome depends far more on how well the accounting channel is executed than on any fixed product characteristic. This reinforces the central thesis of all documents: Ágio's risk is execution risk, not market risk.

No projection is reality, and listing what it leaves out is part of using it honestly. The 30% activation is the most fragile assumption, because it comes from operations in companies with quite different profiles from the SMEs Ágio targets, not primary data with the target population, which only comes in Sprint 4. FIDC capital cost depends on QI Tech confirmation, still pending. The accounting firm acquisition curve assumes a pace that no real conversation has yet validated. And the terminal value, however conservative the multiple, rests on the hypothesis that the operation stays healthy beyond the modeled horizon. Each of these hypotheses is a point where the spreadsheet might be wrong, and having them explicit is what lets you know where to look when reality starts diverging from the model. The spreadsheet isn't a promise. It's an instrument for discovering, as early as possible, which assumption doesn't hold.
