# Business Model Canvas

The Business Model Canvas is a snapshot of the business model at the present moment — it is not strategy (that piece is closed in Strategic Positioning) nor projection (that is in the spreadsheet). It is the photograph: who each participant is, what they give and receive, and where the operation sustains itself.

For Ágio, the canvas makes visible the asymmetry that organizes everything. The right side (customer) is simple — three segments, one channel, one revenue source. The left side (operations) is where the density lies — five resources, four activities, four partners. This is not an aesthetic choice. It is the thesis of the project translated into a quadrant: Ágio is a distribution operation that carries a financial product inside, and the defensible asset is on the left. A competitor copies the value proposition in three months; rebuilding the network of accounting offices on the left takes years.


> **![\[bcm.png\]](https://res.cloudinary.com/dloj4cl3v/image/upload/v1780941350/bmc_agio_sprint4_1_an2lzs.jpg)**

**Customer segments.** Three segments with distinct roles in the model, and this structure is unusual: most B2B2C fintechs have end customer + channel (two segments), while Ágio has end customer (CLT worker), commercial partner (accounting office) and intermediary channel (SME). The worker pays the fee, the accounting office receives commission, the SME neither pays nor receives — it just authorizes the benefit. Each segment needs to have its need met for the model to run, and treating all three as one is the error that kills the sale.

**Value proposition.** The quadrant shows three separate propositions, one per segment, rather than one overarching proposition. For the worker, the pitch is R$ 9.90 against the R$ 25 revolving credit, without credit scoring and without an app. For the accounting office, it is recurring revenue over existing portfolio, with no operational cost and no credit risk. For the SME, it is turnover reduction measured in termination cost (three to four salaries per employee), not well-being as abstraction. The three conversations have different natures and confusing them weakens all three.

**Channels.** A single channel, and this exclusivity is deliberate decision, not resource limitation. Direct sales to SME breaks the trust of the accounting office, which immediately understands that it would be used as an entry door to later be disintermediated. Paid media to the worker inflates the CAC and cannibalizes the unit margin of R$ 2.77 that sustains break-even at ten accounting offices. Without direct sales and without media: the acquisition of the end consumer is absorbed by the accounting office and the employer, keeping the worker's CAC close to zero.

**Customer relationships.** Three modalities. With the worker, zero human contact — self-service via link and CPF, because any human support scaled to 36 thousand users in Year 5 destroys unit margin. With the accounting office, direct relationship with dedicated support in the first months, because it is exactly in that window that channel churn happens if integration stumbles. With the SME, relationship mediated by the accounting office, with no direct Ágio contact under normal conditions — direct entry disauthorizes the commercial partner, and that is the first thing the accounting office perceives.

**Revenue sources.** A single flow in the first five years: fixed fee of R$ 9.90 per advance, charged from the worker and discounted in the following month's payroll. Net revenue for Ágio after 25% commission: R$ 7.43 per transaction. Cross-sell of payroll loans, insurance and pension is deferred to phase 3 (year 5 onwards), when the network of accounting offices is consolidated enough to justify operational complexity. Adding product before that disperses focus at the moment when focus is the scarcest resource.

**Key resources.** Five resources, and the order matters. The first — network of active accounting offices — is the only truly defensible asset, what you don't buy or replicate in months. Integration with QI Tech, working capital and technical platform are replaceable inputs: there is another BaaS, another source of capital, another stack. The fifth — accumulated knowledge about how Domínio, Totvs and Senior structure payroll data — is what separates an Ágio operating for two years from a competitor starting out, and that difference doesn't appear in any deck.

**Key activities.** Four activities, and the first (activate accounting offices) is what defines the growth rhythm of the entire business. The other three are operational: onboarding executes what activation contracted, monthly cycle keeps revenue running, working capital management prevents cash flow from breaking before break-even. Product activities — engineering of new features, R&D, design — deliberately do not appear on the quadrant because, at this stage, they are not business constraints.

**Key partnerships.** Four partners with distinct natures. Accounting office is the commercial partner with interest aligned through the recurring commission mechanism: the accounting office's revenue grows with product adoption, so it has incentive to expand within the portfolio. QI Tech is critical infrastructure supplier with moderate-high power over Ágio, mitigated by multi-sourcing (Celcoin, Dock and Swap as plausible alternatives). Angel investor enters phase 1 with R$ 100k, without operational weight. FIDC is the source of working capital in the scaling phase, when the initial capital no longer covers the stock of outstanding advances.

**Cost structure.** Three natures. Variable cost per transaction (R$ 4.66), of which more than half is FIDC capital cost (R$ 2.46), pending confirmation from QI Tech — any movement here changes break-even. Proportional commission to the accounting office (R$ 2.48 per transaction, 25% of gross fee), by construction indexed to revenue. Fixed costs grow in three steps over the twelve months — R$ 200/month in validation with founder solo, R$ 9,500/month with first accounting offices entering, R$ 18,500/month in expansion — and the transition between steps is triggered by operational milestones, not by calendar. Gross margin over net revenue is R$ 2.77 per transaction (37% over net, 28% over gross), sufficient for operational break-even at approximately ten accounting offices in full operation.

**Conclusion.** The canvas serves as a map, not as argument. The reading it forces is one: the complexity of Ágio is concentrated on the operations side and the customer side is deliberately simple. Whoever looks at the quadrant looking for product innovation won't find it — the product is validated by five years of Mêntore Bank and is replicable in three months by any fintech with minimum capital. The innovation is in the choice of channel and in the discipline of not running from it, and that discipline is exactly what the reduced right side of the quadrant materializes.

# Business model in prose — channels, partnerships, key activities, key resources

The BMC closed the snapshot of the model. This section unfolds into prose the four pieces that carry the operations behind the quadrant. The order here is not the canvas order — it starts in Channels because it is the strategic decision that organizes the rest, follows into Partnerships because that is where the channel becomes contract, then Activities because that is what the channel demands daily, and closes in Resources because that is what sustains the activities. Read in this sequence, the four are umbilical consequences of a single strategic choice, and that coherence is the health test of the model.

## Channels

Ágio has one channel: accounting offices. It is not repressed diversification due to resource scarcity. It is a strategic decision that organizes everything that comes after.

Three channel alternatives were explicitly refused, and each has its own rejection mechanism. Direct sales to SME breaks the accounting office's trust, which understands in seconds that it is being used as an entry door to later be disintermediated — the channel dries up the moment that risk appears. Additionally, the CAC of direct sales to SMEs of 10 to 100 employees doesn't close the account: the revenue ticket per company is too small to absorb commercial visit, follow-up and individual onboarding. Paid media to the worker inflates the CAC beyond unit margin — with R$ 2.77 of gross margin per transaction and 1.5 transactions/month/user, the monthly LTV per active user is approximately R$ 4.16, which doesn't absorb even R$ 50 of paid CAC in a reasonable horizon. Horizontal benefit platforms in the style of Caju/Flash require the SME to pay for the benefit, which brings back the problem of direct sales to small SMEs.

The channel works in a sequence of four steps. Ágio signs with an accounting office, which has a portfolio of 50 to 200 SMEs with already established trust. The accounting office presents the product to SMEs in its portfolio as a zero-cost benefit, and the sale there is made by the accounting office, not by Ágio. The SME authorizes, the accounting office uploads monthly payroll, Ágio generates link and CPF for employees, the employee advances in three clicks and receives via Pix, and the next payroll automatically discounts. The accounting office receives 25% of the fee in monthly recurring commission, and that commission is what keeps the channel interested in expanding adoption within the portfolio instead of just implementing and forgetting.

The reality of the channel was partially tested in Sprint 3, and what it showed is that cold outreach via Google Maps returned 9% (21 contacts, 2 responses, 0 meetings scheduled), while outreach via personal network returned 57% (7 contacts, 4 responses, 2 meetings confirmed for Sprint 4). The reading is direct: the first ten accounting offices will come from personal network and chained referral, not from cold prospecting. CAC per accounting office in the first twelve months is measured in founder time, not in money — and that is why solo operation works in the validation phase but not in phase 2. From the second year onwards, with documented cases that work as social proof, cold outreach passes to having acceptable return rate. It is not a permanent window, it is a stage.

## Key partnerships

Four partner categories with distinct natures and different power dynamics.

**Accounting office.** Primary commercial partner. Receives 25% of the fee per transaction, equivalent to R$ 2.48 per advance. It is the only partner with interest aligned through the recurring commission mechanism — the accounting office's revenue grows monotonically with product adoption in the portfolio of SMEs. Models with one-shot commission (partner sells, receives payment, disappears) create partners who don't come back to defend the product when internal resistance appears. Ágio's recurring commission model creates a partner that has financial reason month to month to defend and expand adoption. Risk from the accounting office side: structurally conservative profile, which resists any move that might shake the trust of SMEs in the portfolio. Mitigation lies in low-friction integration (CSV upload post-closing, discount report ready to download, zero change in existing payroll process) and in sales argument based on numbers — termination cost of three to four salaries per employee, expected revenue of R$ 1,875/month commission over portfolio of 500 active employees —, not in well-being as abstraction.

**QI Tech.** Critical infrastructure supplier. Provides licensing as banking correspondent, KYC, antifraud, Pix processing at institutional volume and (in the scaling phase) FIDC. Partner's power over Ágio: moderate-high. Without QI Tech or equivalent, Ágio does not operate legally — would require own Central Bank license, with regulatory capital and legal structure outside the scope of phase 1. Mitigation through multi-sourcing: Celcoin, Dock and Swap are viable alternatives for BaaS/KYC; FIDC can come from independent managers like Hike Capital or BRA. Ágio's technical architecture is kept agnostic to supplier so that migration is a matter of re-integration, not redesign. Critical pending item at this moment: confirmation of FIDC capital cost by QI Tech — the financial model uses R$ 2.46 per transaction as estimate, and the formal response to the email sent hasn't come yet. Any movement in this number displaces break-even.

**Angel investor.** Phase 1 capital. R$ 100k of initial contribution plus R$ 3k from founder cover operation until operational break-even, projected for the end of Year 1. Investor's power: high at moment of contribution (no capital, no operation), declining as model reaches positive EBITDA. Does not make operational decisions by contract design, but has direct interest in traction milestones (number of active accounting offices with relevant volume, frequency per user, actual activation rate). Expected profile: investor with experience in B2B2C fintech, in regulated markets, or in the accounting universe itself — someone who understands that the accounting channel takes time and that TAM of R$ 6.4 million in Year 5 is not ambition failure, it is model coherence. Not the weekend angel behind unicorn.

**FIDC.** Working capital from the scaling phase, from Year 2-3 onwards. Each advance immobilizes capital for approximately 25 days between the Pix to the worker and the discount in the following month's payroll. With the growth of operations, the average stock outstanding exceeds initial capital at some point between months 8 and 10, and from then on the FIDC enters as a source of working capital. Typical structure: FIDC senior backed by the advances themselves, with cost between CDI+2% and CDI+3% (approximately 18% per annum with Selic at 15%), managed by independent manager, with Ágio maintaining subordinated quota. Risk: dependence on a single manager creates single point of failure; the strategy is to diversify between two or three FIDCs from the volume that supports the structure.

## Key activities

Four activities, ordered by impact on business velocity.

**Activate accounting offices.** The activity that defines the growth rhythm of everything. Each accounting office activated opens access to 50-200 SMEs and approximately 1,500 employees. But "activate" is not "close contract" — it is to make the accounting office actually operate the product in the portfolio, and the distance between signature and actual operation is typically 4 to 8 weeks. The activity includes prospecting (via personal network and referral in phase 1), presentation, demonstration with simulation in the accounting office's financial spreadsheet, contracting, technical integration with test CSV, team training, and communication to the first batch of SMEs. The honest metric is accounting office with more than one hundred monthly transactions, not accounting office that signed. Confusing one with the other is the error that inflates deck and breaks projection.

**Accounting office and SME onboarding.** Operationalizes what activation contracted. Includes setting up the accounting office in the dashboard, uploading the first payroll, validating limit calculation per employee, generating access link for each SME and communicating to the employee about how to access and use. The first monthly cycle is part of onboarding — before this cycle closes clean, the accounting office is not truly operating. The activity demands direct attention from the founder in phase 1 and migrates to a Customer Success position from the tenth accounting office onwards, when the curve is already documented enough to be executed by non-founder.

**Monthly cycle operation.** Recurring activity that keeps revenue running. For each active accounting office, in each month: receive CSV payroll upload post-closing, process and validate data, calculate limit per employee in new cycle, process advance requests (authorization, Pix in seconds, recording), generate discount report for next payroll. Almost everything is automated. The part that demands human attention is exception resolution — CPF without link, employee in trial period, limit adjustment. Estimated volume of exceptions per active accounting office in stabilized operation: one to three per month.

**Manage working capital.** Activity that prevents cash flow from breaking before break-even. With 6,075 advances projected in month 12 and average ticket of R$ 200, the average stock of outstanding advances stays in the order of R$ 1 million — the contribution of R$ 103k doesn't cover that, and the FIDC needs to be in place before stock exceeds cash. The activity includes weekly forecast of capital need, negotiation with capital supplier (angel in phase 1, FIDC in phase 2+), reconciliation between inflows and outflows, and monitoring of delinquency — the 0.2% from Mêntore Bank is reference of the mechanism, not guarantee for Ágio, and each accounting office can have deviation that needs to be observed.

What deliberately does not appear in the list of key activities at this stage: product development. Engineering of new features, R&D, advanced design — none of that is a business constraint while the channel network is not consolidated. The product is validated by five years of Mêntore Bank and is replicable in three months by any fintech with minimum capital. Investing in product differentiation before having channel is misallocation. Product activity enters phase 3 (year 5+), and even there it will be for cross-sell, not for the advance itself.

## Key resources

Five resources, ordered by defensibility. The order matters because the business defensibility is monotonic in this list — whoever copies the fifth resource doesn't copy the first.

**Network of active accounting offices.** The only truly defensible asset. It is not "number of accounting offices signed" — it is number of accounting offices operating the product with relevant volume. Grows month to month through composition of switching cost: each accounting office invests 4 to 8 weeks to integrate, train team and communicate to portfolio of SMEs. Leaving Ágio for a competitor requires redoing those weeks, and the accounting office only does that if there is concrete reason. The defensibility compounds includes direct relationship between Ágio and each accounting office (each has objections, preferences and own rhythms, and Ágio learns that over the first months), accumulated knowledge about the profile of each one, and incentive alignment through recurring commission that makes the accounting office resist switching suppliers without reason.

**Integration with QI Tech (or equivalent).** Infrastructure resource, replaceable by design. Provides licensing as banking correspondent, KYC, antifraud, FIDC and Pix at institutional volume. Without that layer, Ágio would need its own Central Bank license — regulatory capital, legal structure, timeline measured in years — which is outside the scope of phase 1. The integration is technical (APIs) and contractual (banking correspondent), and replaceability is deliberately maintained: Ágio's stack has no deep dependencies on QI Tech that prevent migration to Celcoin, Dock or Swap in a matter of weeks.

**Working capital.** Direct input of operations, not built capacity. Each R$ 200 advance immobilizes R$ 200 for 25 days until discount in payroll. The initial contribution of R$ 103k covers operations until the average stock outstanding exceeds that capital — projected for months 8 to 10. From then on, FIDC replaces the contribution as a source of capital. The resource is replaceable by nature — it can come from additional contribution, bank debt, FIDC or credit line — and the choice is cost optimization, not capacity building.

**Technical platform.** Current stack: Node.js 20, Express, SQLite with planned migration to PostgreSQL as volume justifies. It is not a source of competitive advantage. Any fintech with two months of development replicates the stack. The resource enters the list because it is necessary to operate, not because it protects. The technical decision is to keep the stack as simple as possible to minimize maintenance cost until volume justifies investment in more robust architecture — premature optimization here is waste because the constraint is not technological.

**Accumulated industry knowledge.** Resource that doesn't appear in any deck but is what separates an Ágio operating for two years from a competitor starting out. Includes how Domínio, Totvs and Senior structure payroll data (each one different, with specific traps), the seasonality of monthly closing and the impact on flow, the language of the accounting office owner versus the language of the assistant who operates the upload, typical objections at each stage of the conversation, the profile of SME that accepts the benefit quickly versus the profile that resists. It is not codified in document (nor should it be, because it becomes commodity the moment it leaks), but it is what reduces the time to activate a new accounting office as operations accumulate history. It is equivalent to a learning curve that the competitor redoes from scratch.

---

**Conclusion.** Ágio's operational side is organized around a single strategic decision — exclusive distribution via accounting office — and the four pieces above are consequences of that decision. Channels defines who Ágio approaches and who it doesn't approach. Partnerships defines how the channel is remunerated and protected against supplier capture. Activities defines where the attention of the founder (and the team that enters later) goes daily. Resources defines what needs to be in place for activities to work. The coherence between the four is the health test of the model: activity without resource that sustains it, partnership without clear incentive alignment, or channel that demands activity that structure can't support — any of these three signals indicates misaligned model. Read in that sequence, the operational model is not simpler than it needs to be, and not more complex than it can sustain at this stage.

# Revenue structure and detailed pricing

## The structural choice: fixed fee, not interest

Ágio's revenue comes from a single source in the current phase: the fee charged to the worker at each advance. Charged once per transaction, discounted from the following month's payroll, without monthly interest and without a component proportional to the amount advanced. The choice to charge fixed rather than variable is deliberate for three reasons.

The first is simplicity for the user. The worker earning up to three minimum wages is not in a position to evaluate one monthly interest rate against another — is looking to know how much they will pay. R$ 9.90 fixed gives immediate answer on the screen before confirmation, and that answer reduces friction in the usage flow and increases the probability of conversion on first attempt.

The second is regulatory framing. Charging fixed fee per advance allows positioning the product as early access to already-earned salary, not as a loan. Operating as a loan requires specific license, stricter prudential provisioning, Central Bank reporting. Operating as salary advance via fintech banking correspondent partner (QI Tech) is the lightest path regulatorily, and flat pricing sustains that framing.

The third is defensible transparency. The sales argument against revolving card and informal loan sharks is that Ágio's fee doesn't change — doesn't grow over time, doesn't roll to the next month, doesn't compound. That promise becomes harder to keep in a monthly interest model, even if mathematically equivalent, because the perceived appearance is different. The fixed dispenses with the explanation.

## What the market charges today

To evaluate if R$ 9.90 is expensive or cheap, you need to measure against what the target worker pays today when they need R$ 200 before salary hits.

Credit card revolving operates at 445% to 451.5% per annum (Central Bank, Sep/2025), which puts the cost of R$ 200 for 30 days at approximately R$ 25 to R$ 30. Overdraft runs at 134.2% per annum (Central Bank, Apr/2025), cost of approximately R$ 22 in the same 30 days. Non-consigned personal credit, at 106.6% per annum (Central Bank, Sep/2025), costs approximately R$ 18 — but requires score that the operational base negatived rarely has. Informal lending and apps for negative-scored people charge 10% to 15% per month (estimate crossed from industry reports, without official source), which puts R$ 200 for 30 days at R$ 20 to R$ 30 and still generates the rollover risk that makes debt grow.

The most aggressive price competitor is private payroll loan via CTPS Digital, enabled by Law 15.179/2025, with average fee of 58.4% per annum and range of 21% to 79% (Central Bank via Serasa, Sep/2025) — cost of R$ 9 to R$ 13 for R$ 200 in 30 days. In theory, it is a close alternative to Ágio in price. The defense against that substitute is friction, not numerical difference: three cell phone clicks against contracting a contract regulated by the worker's bank.

Other fintechs that charge the worker themselves also serve as reference. Xerpa, in a NeoFeed report from September 2020, charged a fixed fee of R$ 5 per advance with average ticket of R$ 350 — model viable in large companies (iFood, Mercado Livre, CCR, Nubank) where volume sustains low fee. Sólides via Payssego, in institutional material from March 2026, charges a fixed fee of R$ 14.90 per request with Pix in seconds — model closer to Ágio in nature (SME via partner), with higher price justified by less efficient sales.

Reading those references together, the target worker pays today between R$ 9 and R$ 30 for R$ 200 in 30 days. R$ 9.90 sits at the floor of the range, slightly below regulated private payroll loan, and well below what that worker actually accesses when they need money (card, informal, overdraft).

## The fee of R$ 9.90: rationale and sensitivity

The number is not arbitrary. It follows from three simultaneous constraints: needs to be perceived as inferior to alternatives (price anchor below R$ 20), needs to cover direct cost per transaction with margin (direct cost R$ 4.66 + 25% commission to office = R$ 7.14 per transaction, leaving R$ 2.77 gross margin before fixed), and needs to look like "a single note" to reinforce perceived simplicity — the format R$ 9.90 works psychologically better than R$ 8 or R$ 12, and retail price-anchor literature has confirmed that intuition for decades.

What changes if the fee goes up to R$ 12.90. Gross revenue per transaction +30%, unit margin almost doubles (from R$ 2.77 to R$ 5.77 keeping commission at 25%), but the price-anchor argument against alternatives weakens — sits too close to Payssego's R$ 14.90 without the channel differential. Real risk: drop in frequency per user, possibly from 1.5x to something between 1.0 and 1.3. In scenario of high price elasticity in this segment, R$ 12.90 can reduce total revenue per user instead of increasing it.

What changes if the fee goes down to R$ 7.90. Unit margin shrinks to R$ 0.77 (direct cost + commission sum to R$ 7.12), which practically eliminates fixed margin to cover fixed cost. Break-even would rise from 10 accounting offices to something between 18 and 25, depending on frequency elasticity. Not a rational move at this stage: the additional savings for the worker (R$ 2 per transaction) doesn't compensate for the delay in break-even.

The sensitivity is mapped in the spreadsheet (Scenarios tab). In the base scenario — R$ 9.90, 1.5x frequency, R$ 200 ticket, 30% activation — the model delivers break-even at ~10 accounting offices. Moves up or down in fee have cross effect with frequency that needs to be modeled case by case. The chosen number is locally optimal within the tested range, and any revision depends on primary data that Sprint 4 begins to collect.

## Accounting office commission: 25%

Of the R$ 9.90 charged from the worker, R$ 2.48 (25%) goes to the accounting office as monthly recurring commission. Net revenue to Ágio per transaction: R$ 7.43.

The choice of 25% follows market-making logic, not present value calculation. Lower commission (10% to 15%) doesn't create strong enough incentive for the accounting office to expand adoption within the portfolio — the effort of communicating SME by SME doesn't pay off in low volume. Higher commission (35% to 40%) leaves insufficient margin for Ágio to cover fixed cost in the validation phase. The point of 25% is the balance tested in initial conversations of Sprint 3: generates expected revenue of approximately R$ 1,875 per month for an accounting office with 500 active employees at base frequency, number high enough to become serious conversation with the accounting office partner and low enough for Ágio to operate with margin.

The sensitivity varying commission between 20% and 30% makes clear the trade-off. At 20%, R$ 1.98 goes to the office and R$ 7.92 stays net to Ágio, with unit margin rising to R$ 3.26 and break-even falling to approximately 8 accounting offices. But the expected revenue for the office drops to R$ 1,500/month, psychological frontier below which the sales argument weakens and adoption may drop with it. At 30%, R$ 2.97 goes to the office and R$ 6.93 stays net, unit margin at R$ 2.27, break-even rising to approximately 12 accounting offices — but expected office revenue becomes R$ 2,250/month and adoption accelerates. The point of 25% is locally optimal in the tested range, and can be revised from the twentieth accounting office onwards, when real volume replaces hypothetical conversation as calibration base.

## Revenue per transaction, per user and per accounting office

Ágio's revenue geometry is multiplicative, not additive, and the logic becomes clearer at four chained levels.

Per transaction: R$ 9.90 gross, R$ 2.48 to office, R$ 7.43 net to Ágio.

Per active user per month: 1.5 transactions × R$ 7.43 = R$ 11.15 net revenue monthly to Ágio (and R$ 3.72 to office).

Per accounting office in full operation: 1,500 employees in base × 30% steady state activation = 450 active users, × R$ 11.15 = R$ 5,018/month net revenue to Ágio (and R$ 1,673/month commission to office).

Annualizing the steady state per office: R$ 60,225 net revenue to Ágio, R$ 20,075 commission to office. Multiplying by the 9 offices projected at end of Year 1: potential net revenue of R$ 542,025/year in stabilized regime — but Year 1 delivers only R$ 218,859 because most offices enter halfway through the year and the activation curve takes 6 months to mature. The difference between potential steady state and realized in Year 1 is what the spreadsheet calls ramp factor (0.40), and that is the portion of potential that actually becomes revenue in the first year.

Those numbers are all steady state — assume mature activation after 6-month ramp and base average frequency. In the real curve, the first month of each office's operation has 5% activation, and the spreadsheet (Projection_12M tab) models the curve month by month with activation rising in a ramp to 30%.

## Pricing and revenue risks and pending items

Three points can move these numbers.

The FIDC capital cost, estimated at R$ 2.46 per transaction, has not yet been confirmed by QI Tech. Email sent, no response until this section closes. If it comes above R$ 2.46, unit margin shrinks proportionally and break-even moves. To calibrate sensitivity: R$ 3.00 capital cost (R$ 0.54 increase over estimate) would drop unit margin from R$ 2.77 to R$ 2.23, and break-even would rise from 10 to approximately 12 accounting offices. R$ 3.50 would bring break-even to 15.

The 1.5x monthly frequency is Mêntore Bank operation reference in companies with quite different profile than Ágio's target (more employees, higher average income profile, corporate urban context). In traditional SMEs with operational base, real frequency could be higher (tighter financial margin, more acute need) or lower (digital barrier, low familiarity with recurring financial product). First primary validation is in the two Sprint 4 meetings.

The 30% steady state activation is estimate based on market references (Gibb, Mêntore). In SMEs without prior culture of digital financial benefit, it could be well below. The elasticity of this premise on total revenue is high: drop to 20% activation reduces revenue per office by a third, and moves break-even to something between 14 and 16 accounting offices. It is the most fragile premise in the model and the one that most deserves empirical attention in the coming weeks.

## Conclusion

The revenue structure of Ágio is deliberately simple — one fixed fee, one single source, two outflows (R$ 7.43 to Ágio, R$ 2.48 to office). Simplicity is not aesthetic virtue; it is operational prerequisite. Complex pricing in an operational base segment increases friction, reduces first-attempt conversion, increases support costs and delays the revenue curve. The R$ 9.90 are locally optimal for the modeled base scenario, and the sensitivity of the three premises that sustain revenue — capital cost, frequency, activation — is mapped and under validation. Net revenue of R$ 7.43 per transaction is the number that enters the next two sections (cost structure and unit economics), and any movement here propagates downstream with multiplicative effect on break-even.

# Detailed cost structure

## The three cost types

Ágio's operations have three cost natures, and treating them as separate categories is important because each behaves differently as volume grows.

Variable costs per transaction scale linearly with advance volume. Each processed transaction generates a cost that must be paid regardless of revenue, and the set of those costs is not optimizable in the short term — they are function of external suppliers (BaaS, FIDC, Pix) who practice relatively rigid tables at low volumes.

Commission to accounting office (25% over gross revenue, R$ 2.48 per transaction) also scales with volume, but is different from pure variable cost because it is proportional to revenue, not to operations. High commission creates aligned commercial partner; low commission eliminates the channel. For managerial accounting purposes, it sits between variable cost and channel investment — in this document it is treated separately.

Fixed costs don't scale with volume in the relevant operation range. They grow in three discrete steps as operations mature, and each step is triggered by operational milestone, not calendar. This design is deliberate: hiring people before the operation that justifies them burns cash quickly.

## Direct costs per transaction

Each processed advance generates five cost categories. The sum is R$ 4.66 per transaction. The values are estimates based on public market sources and are subject to revision when formal confirmation from QI Tech arrives.

**Capital cost (FIDC): R$ 2.46.** When a worker advances R$ 200, Ágio needs to have R$ 200 available to send. That capital stays immobilized until discount in the following month's payroll, on average 25 days. In the validation phase, capital comes from founder and angel investor contributions. In scale, it comes from FIDC. FIDC senior costs, according to data from Hike Capital and StoneX, run between CDI+2% and CDI+3% per annum. With Selic at 15% in 2025, total cost is approximately 18% per annum. For R$ 200 immobilized for 25 days: R$ 200 × 0.18 × (25/365) = R$ 2.46. This is the most significant direct cost of operations, and any movement here propagates over margin and break-even.

**Pix transfer: R$ 1.00.** Pix for legal entities is charged by financial institutions. According to Paytime survey from March 2026, fees vary from R$ 0.50 to R$ 1.50 per transaction, depending on provider and volume. The model works with conservative average of R$ 1.00. At volume above 10 thousand transactions/month, there is room to negotiate to reduce this cost to R$ 0.70-0.80.

**Backend and cloud infrastructure: R$ 0.40.** Cost of keeping the application running — database, servers, monitoring — varies significantly with volume. In initial scale, the allocation per transaction sits around R$ 0.40. The number drops with scale: at 30 thousand monthly transactions, the allocation per transaction sits at R$ 0.20-0.25.

**KYC and antifraud: R$ 0.30.** Identity verification, bureau consultation, fraud prevention. Charged by BaaS provider. In CLT operational base with payroll data previously validated by accounting office, fraud risk is structurally low, but the cost of the consultation is charged per transaction regardless.

**Allocated support: R$ 0.50.** Proportional allocation of human support cost over transaction volume. Includes time spent on exception resolution (CPF without link, limit adjustment, operational question from office). Estimate based on premise of one to three exceptions per month per office in stabilized operation, with average cost of R$ 30 to R$ 60 per resolved exception.

The sum of five categories gives R$ 4.66 per transaction. Over gross revenue of R$ 9.90, that is 47% direct cost. Over net revenue to Ágio (R$ 7.43 after commission), 63%.

## Fixed costs by phase

Fixed costs grow in three steps over twelve months, and each transition is triggered by operational milestones documented in the spreadsheet.

**Phase 1 — Validation (months 1-3): R$ 200/month.** Lucas operates solo. Costs cover product hosting on Render, Claude Pro Max subscription, domain. No salary, no contractor, no professional services — all activity is executed by founder. Transition trigger: first contract closed with accounting office.

**Phase 2 — First offices (months 4-6): R$ 9,500/month.** First operational reinforcement enters, without expansion of core team. Composition: dev PJ part-time R$ 6,000 (focus on corrections and post-validation product improvements), contracted support R$ 2,500 (office attendance when exceptions appear), infrastructure and software R$ 1,000 (cloud in production, monitoring, management software). Founder continues executing sales, onboarding and main operational activity. Transition trigger: third accounting office in stable operation (volume above 100 transactions/month).

**Phase 3 — Expansion (months 7-12): R$ 18,500/month.** Professional team begins to form. Composition: dev R$ 7,000 (full-time PJ), support R$ 3,000 (PJ or CLT), CS R$ 4,000 (CLT or dedicated PJ), marketing R$ 2,000 (PJ, content and materials), legal and accounting R$ 1,500 (outsourced accounting firm for Ágio itself, plus legal advisory on demand), infrastructure R$ 1,000. From this point onwards, founder stops executing operational tasks and only does what only he can do — strategic decision, sales for larger offices, QI Tech negotiation, FIDC fundraising.

The difference between phase 2 and phase 3 is significant (R$ 9,000/month additional), and that transition is the most sensitive moment of the financial plan. If it happens before operations justify it — before contribution margin covers expanded fixed cost — cash burns fast and break-even delays. If it delays too long, operations get bottlenecked in founder attention and growth stalls. The documented trigger (third office in stable operation) is the way to guarantee that the transition is induced by operational reality, not by calendar.

## Annualized fixed costs

Summing the twelve months:
- Phase 1 (months 1-3): R$ 200 × 3 = R$ 600
- Phase 2 (months 4-6): R$ 9,500 × 3 = R$ 28,500
- Phase 3 (months 7-12): R$ 18,500 × 6 = R$ 111,000

Total Year 1: R$ 140,100.

The spreadsheet (Projection_36M tab) projects R$ 360,000 for Year 2 — reflects team expansion as number of accounting offices rises to 20, with composition of senior dev, two support, CS, marketing and legal. For Year 3, R$ 540,000, reflecting stabilized team of six to eight people plus infrastructure and administrative expenses growing proportionally.

## What is not modeled

Four cost categories are knowingly underestimated or absent from the model, and each deserves to be explicit to avoid surprise.

CAC for accounting office is modeled implicitly as founder time in phase 1. When sales begin to be executed by non-founder (phase 3 onwards), CAC per office needs to be estimated and added to the model. Expected order of magnitude: R$ 2,000 to R$ 5,000 per activated office, considering SDR time, meetings, follow-up and technical onboarding.

Regulatory acquisition costs are zero in the MVP because Ágio operates as QI Tech's banking correspondent without own license. At scale (phase 3 of strategic plan, year 5+), it may make sense to seek SCD (Direct Credit Company) or SEP license. Today: zero. Three to five years from now: estimated range of R$ 200,000 to R$ 500,000 just for incorporation legal work, not counting regulatory capital.

Delinquency is modeled as zero in Year 1 flow, based on Mêntore Bank reference (0.2% over revenue). But Mêntore operates in large companies with different profile. In operational SMEs, terminations between advance and payroll can be more frequent. Prudential reserve recommended from actual volume being measured: 0.5% to 1% over gross revenue.

Variable legal expenses (disputes, unforeseen regulatory adjustments, changes in labor legislation) are not in the fixed line by design — they are contingency. Suggested reserve from Year 2 onwards: 2% over gross revenue.

## Conclusion

Ágio's cost structure is dominated by two factors in very different magnitude: FIDC capital cost (R$ 2.46/transaction, which can move entire unit margin) and the phase 3 transition step (from R$ 9,500 to R$ 18,500/month, which defines when operations leaves founder-solo). The rest of costs is controllable and predictable. The discipline of transitioning between phases by operational trigger, not calendar, is what separates a coherent financial plan from a plan that confuses aspiration with execution. The non-modeled pending items (CAC for office in professionalized phase, actual delinquency, legal reserves) are not small enough to be ignored, and each will be sized as primary data begins to come in.

# Unit economics and break-even

## Why unit economics matters before scale

Before discussing EBITDA, NPV or IRR, it makes sense to look at the number that defines whether the business works or not at the most elementary level: how much is left from each processed transaction, after paying who needs to be paid, and before covering fixed cost. If that number is negative, scale makes the situation worse — each additional transaction deepens loss. If it is positive, scale helps — you just need cumulative volume to cover fixed cost. Unit economics is the test of viability under the microscope before the telescope.

## The geometry per transaction

For each processed advance:

Gross revenue charged from worker: R$ 9.90
(-) Commission to office (25%): R$ 2.48
= Net revenue to Ágio: R$ 7.43

(-) FIDC capital cost: R$ 2.46
(-) Pix: R$ 1.00
(-) Cloud infrastructure: R$ 0.40
(-) KYC and antifraud: R$ 0.30
(-) Allocated support: R$ 0.50
= Total direct cost: R$ 4.66

Gross margin (net revenue – direct cost): R$ 2.77 per transaction.

In percentage: 37% gross margin over Ágio net revenue, 28% over gross revenue charged from worker. Whenever those numbers appear in any document, the calculation base needs to be explicit — 37% over net and 28% over gross are not interchangeable figures.

## Margin per active user

Active user in Ágio's model makes on average 1.5 transactions per month (base frequency). Per active user per month:

Net revenue to Ágio: 1.5 × R$ 7.43 = R$ 11.15
Gross margin: 1.5 × R$ 2.77 = R$ 4.16

LTV depends on retention. If the user stays active for 18 months on average — reasonable premise given that the advance solves recurring need, and that as long as the worker is employed in the SME the function remains available — the LTV in gross margin sits at approximately R$ 75. Worker CAC is close to zero (enters via accounting office-company channel with no direct acquisition cost), which makes LTV/CAC ratio tend to infinity by construction. This is virtue of the channel, not the product, and disappears if the model migrates to direct sales.

## Margin per accounting office in full operation

Accounting office in full operation has approximately 1,500 employees in portfolio, with 30% steady state activation (450 active users).

Per office per month:
Net revenue to Ágio: 450 × R$ 11.15 = R$ 5,018
Gross margin to Ágio: 450 × R$ 4.16 = R$ 1,872
Commission paid to office: 450 × 1.5 × R$ 2.48 = R$ 1,674

Annualizing the steady state per office: net revenue to Ágio of R$ 60,225 and gross margin of R$ 22,464. Annual revenue to office (commission): approximately R$ 20,089. Those numbers are Year 2 onwards, or Year 1 for an office that entered in month 1 and matured the activation ramp.

## Break-even calculation

Operational break-even is the point where gross margin covers the fixed cost of the expansion phase. With R$ 18,500/month fixed and R$ 2.77 margin per transaction:

Transactions at break-even = R$ 18,500 / R$ 2.77 = 6,691 transactions per month.

Converting to active users (at 1.5 transactions per user/month):
Users at break-even = 6,691 / 1.5 = 4,461 active users.

Converting to offices in full operation (at 450 active users per office):
Offices at break-even = 4,461 / 450 = 9.91 offices.

Rounding, break-even is at approximately ten offices in full operation, equivalent to 4,461 active users and 6,691 monthly transactions. That is the number that governs the entire validation phase.

## When break-even is reached

The spreadsheet (Projection_12M tab) models month by month curve. In month 12, the model has 9 active offices (most still in activation ramp, not full operation), 4,050 effective active users and 6,075 transactions in the month. Month 12 EBITDA: –R$ 1,703. Close to monthly break-even, but still negative.

The honest reading is that operational break-even happens in early Year 2, not within Year 1. Year 1 closes with accumulated EBITDA of –R$ 58,599. Year 2 comes in at –R$ 35,251 accumulated (operation positive in several months, recovering part of deficit). Year 3 delivers positive accumulated EBITDA of R$ 75,904.

The NPV considering only the operational flows of the three years is negative at R$ 130,702, and operational IRR doesn't exist as a positive number in that horizon. The reason is simple: three years is too short a timeframe for a business that reaches break-even only at end of Year 1. The spreadsheet shows this number explicitly, without hiding, and adds terminal value (5x Year 3 EBITDA, R$ 379,519) to capture post-break-even perpetuity. With terminal value included, NPV becomes +R$ 118,838 and IRR at 41% per annum. Return on investment (Year 3 EBITDA over total contribution of R$ 103,000) is 73.7% — metric deliberately called return on investment, not ROE, because EBITDA is not net profit and contribution is not equity in strict accounting sense.

## Break-even sensitivity

Three variables move break-even sensibly.

FIDC capital cost. If confirmed by QI Tech in value different from R$ 2.46, unit margin changes and break-even follows. R$ 3.00 capital cost (R$ 0.54 increase over estimate) takes unit margin from R$ 2.77 to R$ 2.23, and break-even rises from 6,691 transactions to 8,296 — equivalent to 12.3 offices in full operation. R$ 3.50 capital cost raises break-even to 15 offices. It is the sharpest sensitivity of the model.

Usage frequency. If real frequency stays at 1.0 transaction/month instead of 1.5 (pessimistic scenario in Scenarios tab), break-even in transactions stays equal (6,691), but number of users necessary rises to 6,691, and number of offices rises to 14.9. Almost 50% more offices to hit the same point.

Activation per office. If real activation stays at 20% instead of 30%, number of active users per office drops from 450 to 300, and number of offices necessary for break-even rises from 9.91 to 14.9. Sensitivity identical in magnitude to frequency.

The adverse combination of all three (R$ 3.00 capital, 1.0 frequency, 20% activation) takes break-even to something between 22 and 28 offices, and payback horizon beyond five years. That is the limit pessimistic scenario, modeled in Scenarios tab, considered coherent with premises still not primarily validated.

## Conclusion

Ágio's unit economics is positive at the base: R$ 2.77 margin per transaction is sufficient for the business to operate and amortize fixed cost in the range of ten offices in full operation. The model has no fundamental problem — it has a time problem. Three years is a short timeframe for a business whose activation curve takes six months per office and whose acquisition curve for offices, in the manual phase, takes months. Year 1 burns cash, Year 2 breaks even, Year 3 begins to yield. The real value only materializes from Year 4 onwards, when fixed cost is stabilized and each new office enters with almost pure margin over already-paid structure. Whoever judges the model only by the first three years is watching the movie before the final act. The spreadsheet shows both sides — operational without terminal value and operational with terminal value — so the reader makes their own judgment, without disguising the discomfort of the short horizon.

# Hiring plan for the first 12 months

## Guiding principle: hiring by trigger, not calendar

Teams in startups tend to be hired before the work that justifies existence, and that is one of the main causes of early cash burn. The plan below resists that temptation by construction — each hire has specific operational trigger, and the structure rises in controlled steps that mirror the three cost phases modeled in the spreadsheet.

## Phase 1 (months 1-3): founder solo

Lucas executes everything: accounting office prospecting and activation, onboarding, monthly cycle operation, support, product development, financial management. No hiring.

Personnel cost: zero. Phase total cost: R$ 200/month (tools).

The rationale is direct: phase 1 work is to validate channel, and validating channel requires direct founder contact with each prospect office. Delegation here would destroy signal — the conversation needs to be in the voice of whoever made the strategic decisions, because the office will test those decisions. Besides, the volume of actual operation is still zero or one, and any hire would be idle capacity.

Transition trigger to phase 2: first contract closed with accounting office, with first payroll upload completed.

## Phase 2 (months 4-6): two part-time PJs

Operations begins to run, and two functions need to exist for founder to stay focused on activating more offices.

**Part-time dev PJ — R$ 6,000/month (approximately 20h/week).** Profile: full-stack mid-level (3 to 5 years experience), Node.js and Express, relational SQL, comfortable with startup monorepo and controlled technical debt. Assignment: MVP bug fixes, improvements to office onboarding flow, strengthen CSV upload (validation, error messages, recovery), implement automated tests where regression hurts most, basic monitoring (structured logs, alerts). The choice of part-time PJ instead of full-time CLT is deliberate: product work volume in phase 2 doesn't fill 40h/week, and CLT at partial volume is waste for both sides. Hiring method: personal network first, platforms like Toptal or Workana as backup, referral from other founders as third path.

**Contracted support PJ — R$ 2,500/month (approximately 15-20h/week).** Profile: person with B2B customer support experience (not massive call center), clear written Portuguese, patience with conservative-profile offices, familiarity with payroll flows as desirable but not required. Assignment: first-line service when exceptions appear (CPF without link in payroll, limit adjustment, upload question), exception documentation to feed product improvements, maintenance of simple knowledge base for office to consult. Part-time PJ modality with business-hours availability. Hiring method: personal network, LinkedIn, customer success communities.

**Infrastructure and software — R$ 1,000/month.** Not hiring. Covers cloud in production (managed PostgreSQL replacing SQLite, server upgrade), monitoring (UptimeRobot or Datadog minimum tier), backup, internal and office communication tools (Slack, WhatsApp Business), management software (Notion or similar).

Phase 2 total: R$ 9,500/month. Founder remains without formal salary.

Transition trigger to phase 3: third office in stable operation, defined as volume above 100 transactions per month per office consistently.

## Phase 3 (months 7-12): professionalized core of four to five people

Ten offices in full operation require some functions to stop being founder-solo. The structure below is the minimum viable to sustain that operation without growth stalling on limited attention of a single person.

**Full-time dev PJ — R$ 7,000/month.** Same profile as phase 2, now full-time. Usually the same professional who entered in phase 2 being scaled to exclusive dedication — continuity reduces transition cost (already knows code, product, users, traps). Expanded assignment: beyond maintenance, begins implementing features that unlock growth — integration with payroll ERPs (Domínio, Totvs, Senior each with particularities), automation of recurring exceptions, analytics dashboard for office.

**Full-time support — R$ 3,000/month (PJ or CLT).** Phase 2 professional scaled, ideally. Expanded assignment: beyond attending exceptions, trains office team during onboarding, documents operational procedures, identifies low-adoption offices to flag to CS. Modality depends on professional preference and volume: PJ works at medium volume, CLT makes sense if volume demands full integral dedication.

**Customer Success Manager — R$ 4,000/month (CLT or dedicated PJ).** New function, with no precedent in phase 2. Profile: 2 to 3 years in customer success in B2B fintech or in accounting consultancy. Ability to talk both to office partner (strategic conversation, ROI, expansion) and to operational assistant (execution conversation, configuration, exception). Articulate in spreadsheets, comfortable with data, consultative profile. Assignment: monitor adoption in each active office via operational metrics, identify offices at churn risk before churn happens (usage drop, delayed upload, above-normal exceptions), execute expansion plan within each portfolio (more portfolio SMEs being activated, more employees per SME), generate channel health reports for founder weekly. The reason this function exists now: founder attention limit is between 10 and 15 offices; above that, churn begins to happen from lack of attention, and CSM is the function that creates sustainable human scale.

**Marketing — R$ 2,000/month (PJ).** Profile: person with B2B content expertise (LinkedIn, blog, newsletter for accounting offices), not growth/performance marketing. The channel is organic and by referral, not paid, so marketing at this stage is authority building and production of sales materials that the office uses with SMEs — not optimization of paid funnel. Assignment: create materials office uses to present Ágio to SMEs (one-pagers, SME ROI calculator, first case studies from active offices), manage presence in accountant communities (LinkedIn, forums like Contábeis and Jornal Contábil), first newsletter for prospect offices.

**Legal and accounting — R$ 1,500/month (outsourced firm).** Not direct hiring. Is fee for an accounting firm to handle Ágio's own accounting (the paradox is deliberate — Ágio's client is the accounting office, but Ágio is also client of one, and having a partner in that universe gives additional channel knowledge) plus legal advisory on demand. Assignment: Ágio's tax obligations, contracts with partner accounting offices, contract with QI Tech or equivalent, monitoring of regulatory changes in salary advance and consigned lending.

**Infrastructure — R$ 1,000/month.** Continuation from phase 2 with no structural change.

Phase 3 total: R$ 18,500/month. Lucas remains without formal salary, with pro-labore minimum if necessary for tax purposes (R$ 1,500 to R$ 2,000/month, reported but recycled into operations).

## The founder in phase 3

Lucas's assignment changes qualitatively: stops doing direct onboarding (passes to CSM and support), stops doing first-line support (passes to support), stops coding (passes to dev), and focuses exclusively on sales to larger offices (that demand founder interlocution to close), negotiation with QI Tech and management of critical supplier relationship, FIDC fundraising for scaling phase, and strategic decision on business direction. That is the only way to sustain growth beyond ten offices — any other arrangement bottlenecks on Lucas.

## Years 2 and 3 — expansion

Detailing of roles for years 2 and 3 depends on actual operation performance and is outside Sprint 4 scope. The bases exist:

Year 2 (R$ 360,000/year, R$ 30,000/month equivalent, aligned with 20 active offices): senior dev migrates to CLT (R$ 10,000/month), support expands to two people (R$ 8,000 total), CS evolves to R$ 5,000, marketing to R$ 3,000, legal to R$ 2,000, infrastructure to R$ 2,000.

Year 3 (R$ 540,000/year, R$ 45,000/month equivalent, with 35 active offices): team expands to 8 to 10 people, with addition of channels head (R$ 10,000) who assumes direct relationship with larger offices, data analyst (R$ 6,000) for portfolio management, second dev (R$ 8,000) and dedicated QA (R$ 4,000).

The specific composition needs revision every quarter, based on which function is bottlenecking growth at that moment.

## CLT vs PJ — logic by role, not philosophy

CLT is better when work is continuous at full volume (40h/week), when professional needs exclusive dedication (CS, senior dev in stabilized phase, support attending in fixed business hours), when company wants to retain knowledge via non-compete clauses and IP ownership, and when charges offset the predictability (total cost ~70% above gross salary, but deductible and stable).

PJ is better when work is variable (consulting, project, part-time), when professional operates with multiple clients (freelance support, content marketing), when company doesn't yet have volume for full CLT, and when professional prefers flexibility (frequent in dev and design).

For Ágio: phase 2 is 100% PJ because volume doesn't justify CLT. Phase 3 mixes — CSM and support tend toward CLT from exclusive dedication, dev and marketing can stay PJ for flexibility.

## Charges not modeled in spreadsheet

The values above are gross fees (PJ) or gross salaries (CLT). For CLT, charges add approximately 70% over gross (employer INSS 20%, FGTS 8%, proportional vacation, 13th month, transportation voucher, meal voucher). For PJ, direct company charges are zero, but effective cost includes tax paid by PJ that becomes charged price (already embedded in the fee).

The current financial model uses the values as they appear in the spreadsheet. When migrating from PJ to CLT in any role, nominal cost rises, and that migration needs to be modeled when it gets close — a PJ position at R$ 6,000 becomes approximately R$ 10,000 in total CLT cost, and the spreadsheet needs to absorb that difference without distorting break-even.

## Conclusion

The hiring plan for the first twelve months is deliberately conservative. Phase 1 is Lucas solo because the work is to validate channel, and channel validation is non-delegable work. Phase 2 adds two PJs because product needs to stabilize and support needs to exist, but real volume doesn't yet justify exclusive dedication. Phase 3 builds the core of four to five people because ten offices in full operation require CS, dedicated support, dev at continuous maintenance rate and marketing to sustain prospect pipeline. The jump between phase 2 and phase 3 doubles monthly fixed cost, and that is why the transition trigger is operational (third stable office), not calendar. Founder remains without formal salary in all three phases because contribution capital is allocated to operations, not remuneration — that is the burden of the validation phase and the justification for angel contribution being R$ 100k not R$ 300k. Each hire has specific profile, specific trigger and contractual modality chosen by function, not by ideological preference. The coherence between team composition and operation stage is the health test of the plan.
